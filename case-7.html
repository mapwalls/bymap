Case Study 05

Parabellum Finance

Every screen in this product ends in a wire transfer. I turned a fragmented, spreadsheet-dependent finance back office into one guided workflow, invoice → payment → reconciliation → reporting, where the interface prevents costly errors instead of relying on people to catch them.

IndustryLitigation finance · Fund accounting
ClientParabellum Capital, via Singular
RoleSenior Product Designer
PlatformWeb (internal platform)

The challenge

Parabellum Capital is a New York litigation funder that manages several funds and pays law firms, experts and service providers on every case it backs. Each payment hides a chain of work: checking an invoice against the case budget, building a payment ticket, verifying wire instructions, matching bank movements and closing the month for each fund. Most of it ran on spreadsheets, scattered Airtable views and memory.

What it looked like

A request for a UI refresh

The first ask was to make the screens look better and more consistent.

What it really was

No process the users could see

The screens had no clear starting point and no sequence, and the risky steps were spread across separate tools. Before redesigning anything, I mapped the real workflow with the team in a Buildathon walkthrough of the payment, banking, reconciliation and reporting screens.

Stage 01

Designing around risk, not just usability

The users are accounting and AP teams who handle large sums of money. In fintech, a good UX decision is one that makes the wrong action hard:

1

A send-readiness checklist. The payment package can't be generated until every check passes.

2

Versioned wire instructions. Each version shows who verified it, when, and when it needs re-verification, answering the question that matters most: is this account still correct?

3

Callback owner and backup. Two different people, by rule, so no single person can confirm a wire alone.

4

Suggested bank matches. Each match shows a confidence level and the day gap between records. The system proposes; the accountant confirms.

Stage 02

Simplifying the structure

Fewer places to go, and fewer decisions about where to go, is a product decision, not just a visual one.

1

Navigation built around the work. Operation, Reporting and Settings, with invoicing as a sequence: Inbox, then Payment Tickets, then Release & Reconcile.

2

One flow for every invoice. Manual entry moved inside the Invoice Inbox, next to the document, the captured fields and the live case budget.

3

One place to approve. AP Management became the Pending approval tab inside the Payment Ticket Builder.

4

What I said no to. Modals only for short flows; anything longer gets its own screen or side panel.

Before

AP Management, a screen of its own

Open invoices lived on a separate screen, one more place to visit before a payment could be built.

After

Pending approval, inside the ticket builder

The same invoices now sit in the first tab of the Payment Ticket Builder. Select invoices from the same vendor and fund and they become one ticket, without leaving the screen.

Stage 03

Designed outcomes

The work supports the client's goal of a reliable investment data foundation with no spreadsheet dependency. The release is still in QA, so these are the outcomes the design is built for, and how each one will be measured:

Designed outcomeHow it will be measured
Fewer payment errorsTickets sent back or corrected before they reach the fund admin
Faster invoice processingTime from receiving an invoice to an approved payment ticket
Less manual reconciliationShare of bank rows matched from suggestions vs. by hand
No spreadsheet dependencyShare of new Fund IV investments created in the platform (the client's key result: 100%)
Consistency13 screens moved from local styles to shared components (done)

Still open with the client: whether the happy path should support batch processing at higher volumes, or stay one invoice at a time.
